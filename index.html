Marginalia

College asks first-year students to find, weigh, and cite sources on their own. A national study found a wide gap between the Google-centric search skills many freshmen bring from high school and what college-level research demands. Marginalia closes that gap one class at a time by making research engaging, humanistic, and, one hopes, fun!

The Patrons

Patron: English Language Arts

  • Lead sample

    Hemingway Didn't Say That

    Students take a famous quotation, the kind that circulates on posters and social media, and trace it to its source. Many trails end somewhere surprising. Garson O'Toole's Quote Investigator models the method, and his book Hemingway Didn't Say That supplies the cases. Students then practice a habit that college writing demands: quote accurately, attribute honestly, cite fully.

  • Who Borrowed What?

    Princeton's Shakespeare and Company Project rebuilds Sylvia Beach's Paris lending library from its borrowing cards. Students find out what Hemingway, Joyce, and their neighbors actually read. (Shared with French!)

  • Did Shakespeare Really Invent That?

    The Oxford English Dictionary credits Shakespeare with over a thousand English neologisms. Digitized books keep turning up earlier uses. Students test one claim and learn how dictionaries (and reputations!) are built.

  • Read Your Own Essay from a Distance

    Voyant Tools transforms a text into word counts and patterns. Students read a class text this way, then apply the same method to their own drafts, revising with what they find.

Patron: Social Studies

  • Lead sample

    Mapping Inequality

    In the 1930s a federal agency graded American neighborhoods for lending risk and colored the "hazardous" ones red. Essex County has its map. Students read the grades and the agents' original notes for towns they know, then enter a live debate among economists over how much harm the maps themselves have caused (and continue to perpetuate in the present).

  • Read Like a Fact-Checker

    Professional fact-checkers leave a website to find out who is behind it. In a district-wide study, high school students who learned this habit in a few short lessons grew significantly better at judging online sources. We run these lessons as bellringers: quick, easy, simple skill development!

  • The Republic of Letters

    Stanford mapped the letters of Voltaire, Franklin, and Locke. The networks proved more national than the letters themselves suggest. Students test a big, popular idea against corresponding data.

Patron: World Languages

  • Lead sample · Spanish

    Herencia

    The Library of Congress needs help transcribing centuries-old Spanish legal documents. Students puzzle out old handwriting, add to a national collection, and have the option of logging service hours. (No registration required!)

  • Italian

    Fourteen Italies

    Dante counted at least fourteen vernaculars in Italy. A twentieth-century atlas of 1,705 maps, now online, shows how villages across Italy said everyday words. Students hunt the dialects behind modern Italian.

  • Italian

    In the Hand of Dante

    A Newark-born novelist's book about a lost Dante manuscript became a star-studded 2026 film that critics panned (it's awful, truly awful, but kinda enjoyable despite it?). Students read excerpts from Dante, the novel, and some reviews to determine what makes an adaptation fail.

  • Italian

    The Florentine Republic

    Brown University's Online Tratte lists the officeholders of the Florentine Republic across its 250 years, 1282 to 1532. Students trace who governed Machiavelli's city, observe how often the same families returned, and assess what led to the Republic's demise.

  • French

    Paris Expats

    Sylvia Beach's lending cards record where her members lived. Students map the Lost Generation across Paris, arrondissement by arrondissement, in French and English.

  • French

    Backward Slang

    Verlan flips syllables, and it is older than it looks: a scholar traced backward slang to a letter from the early 1800s. Students follow slang from the schoolyard to the dictionary.

  • French

    France in Numbers

    data.gouv.fr, France's national open data portal, holds tens of thousands of public datasets. Students ask a question about French life and answer it in French, with real numbers.

  • Chinese

    One Script, Many Tongues

    China's first emperor standardized the written script; spoken languages remained myriad. Jing Tsu's Kingdom of Characters explores how the modern state tried to unify both. Students map the dialects behind "Chinese."

  • Chinese

    The Noodle and the Mosque

    Lanzhou beef noodles, hand-pulled and halal, come from China's Hui Muslims. Students follow one bowl through the Silk Road, learn about Islam in China, and explore some of the shops now found nationwide.

  • Chinese

    China in Africa

    Boston University tracks every Chinese loan commitment to African governments since 2000 (more than 1,300 of them!) in a free download. Students test headlines against the data, with Deborah Brautigam's The Dragon's Gift as a guide. Comparisons between Eastern and Western ventures abroad beckon...
